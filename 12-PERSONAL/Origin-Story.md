# Origin Story

#personal #biography

> The full story of how a kid from University City ended up building AI tools for financial advisors.

---

## San Diego Kid

I was born in San Diego, California. First few years were in North Park — a historic neighborhood just northeast of Balboa Park, founded in 1893, full of Craftsman bungalows and tree-lined parkways. In the early 2000s when I was there, North Park was still transitional — cheap rents, independent businesses, raw character. It's since gentrified into one of San Diego's most desirable neighborhoods (Forbes named it one of America's best hipster neighborhoods in 2012, median home prices now ~$885K). My parents moved us to University City when I was about three. That's where I grew up.

University City — locals call it "UTC" — sits in San Diego's "Golden Triangle," about 13 miles north of downtown. Developed in the 1960s alongside UC San Diego, it's a community of ~50,000 people with a median household income of $117,310 (top 15% nationally). Median age is 31–32, skewed young by the student population. It borders La Jolla to the west, Sorrento Valley's tech corridor to the north, and Miramar to the east. Westfield UTC mall, good public schools, biotech employers — it's where successful San Diego families raise their kids.

That's where I grew up. We were well off. My dad Anthony was a financial advisor at Morgan Stanley, my mom Colleen kept everything running. My sister McKenna was my built-in rival and best friend.

## The Sports Years

I was a sports kid — not in a casual "signed up for rec league" way, but in a competitive, year-round, travel-team way.

**Soccer** was the main sport. San Diego has one of the most competitive youth soccer scenes in the country — clubs like SD Surf (est. 1977, 132 teams, 2,147 players, ECNL member), Albion SC (150+ teams, had the #1-ranked BU16 team in the nation), and Nomads SC. With the arrival of San Diego FC (MLS expansion, 2025 season at Snapdragon Stadium), the pipeline from youth to pro is now complete. I played competitively for years. It taught me how to work within a system, how to compete, and how to lose without quitting.

**Baseball and basketball** filled the other seasons. I wasn't going pro in any of them, but I was good enough to compete and learn what it feels like to be on a team that expects to win.

**Dirt biking** was the wild card. I rode a Honda CRF 150R — that's not a casual trail bike. The CRF 150R is a purpose-built motocross race bike: 149.7cc liquid-cooled single-cylinder four-stroke, 23.5 HP at 12,500 RPM, 11.7:1 compression ratio, Showa suspension. It competes in the Supermini class against 85cc two-strokes and is explicitly for "advanced youth motocross riders." Retail: $5,399. I got sponsors. I was competitive in AMA-sanctioned amateur motocross racing. The dirt bike world taught me something the team sports didn't: when you're on the gate, it's just you. You win or lose alone. That energy carried into entrepreneurship.

**Wrestling** rounded it out. Short season, maximum intensity. Good preparation for negotiation, honestly.

## The Entrepreneurial Spark

There was no single moment. No lemonade stand origin story. I just started building things.

In my early teens, I was running Shopify stores. Not because someone taught me or because I read a book about it — it just made sense to me. You find a product, you build a store, you run ads, you make money. The mechanics of commerce clicked for me the way math clicks for some people.

That was the first signal: I think in systems. I see how things connect. I see where value flows and where it gets stuck.

---

## Sneaker Bot Era

High school is when things got real. I got into sneaker botting — running automated software to purchase limited-release sneakers before they sold out, then reselling them at markup.

The bots I ran: **Bath**, **Trickle Wrath**, **Mech**, and others. Each one was a different tool with different strengths — some were faster on Nike SNKRS, some worked better on Shopify-based drops, some had better proxy support. Running them was a technical operation: you needed the right proxies, the right server speed, the right timing, the right payment profiles. It was a technology business disguised as a sneaker hobby.

The money was real. But the education was worth more. I learned:
- **Automation is leverage** — one person with the right tools outperforms a hundred without them
- **Speed wins** — milliseconds matter, preparation is everything
- **Supply/demand is universal** — the same arbitrage exists in every market
- **Technology is the multiplier** — whoever has better tools wins

This is the exact same instinct I bring to Advisor Intelligence. Advisors are doing manually what technology could automate. I've been automating manual processes since I was 15.

## The Mark Linder Connection

In high school, I showed Mark Linder what I was doing with the bots. He was impressed — not by the sneakers, but by the systems thinking. A high school kid running automated purchasing operations across multiple platforms, managing proxies and servers, scaling a resale business. That's not normal.

That impression led to Fowler Scholars. Mark connected me to the program at SDSU, and that changed the trajectory. Fowler Scholars isn't just an honor — it's a network, a set of opportunities, and a stamp of credibility that opens doors.

## SDSU & Fowler Scholars

I'm at SDSU now, Fowler College of Business, studying finance. Class of 2028. Fowler Scholar.

The Fowler College of Business is AACSB-accredited (fewer than 5% of business schools worldwide), ranked 49th among public undergraduate business programs by U.S. News (2025), with the entrepreneurship program at 14th among publics. It was founded in 1955 and renamed after a $25 million endowment from the Fowler family. The alumni network spans 500,000+ people.

Fowler Scholars is a cohort of just **19 students** (freshmen through seniors) — extremely competitive. The program was founded in 2017 and is currently directed by [[Mentor-Mark-Linder|Mark Lindner]]. Every Scholar participates in a year-long project with real companies (Taylor Farms, Hunter Industries, Sprouts) and is paired with a professional mentor. Guest speakers include Nick Konat (President/COO of Sprouts), Troy Tazbaz (Oracle Sr. VP), Pamela Williamson (VP of Salesforce).

Fowler Scholars gave me:
1. **Real-world projects** — Taylor Farms (current), Sprouts (previous). Both focused on Gen Z market strategy — positioning me as the "young person who understands both generations."
2. **Mentor access** — Dan Skiles (Altruist VP, SDSU MBA alum, Fowler Dean's Board) and others in the San Diego business ecosystem. See [[Mentor-Dan-Skiles]].
3. **The Fowler alumni network** — Mark Halby at DLK and Will Jones at Wells Fargo are both SDSU connections.

The SDSU network is also how I connected with my first clients. Mark Halby at DLK is an SDSU alum. Will Jones at Wells Fargo is an SDSU alum. The Fowler network feeds the pipeline.

## The Advisor Intelligence Pivot

I wanted to get into AI businesses. Who doesn't, right? But the key insight was: don't build something big. Stick to what you know. Use your niche advantage. Start small. Build up.

What do I know? I know financial advising — through my dad, through growing up around it, through watching an industry from the inside. I know advisors are older and need help with technology. I know how they talk, what they worry about, what keeps them up at night.

Core insight: **when someone needs help, that means there's money to be made.**

The first meeting with a real advisor confirmed everything. I showed them what AI could do for their workflow — meeting prep, follow-up emails, client communication. They were genuinely impressed. They had never seen it before. That reaction — the surprise, the "this would save me hours" — that's when I knew this was real.
